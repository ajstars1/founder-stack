# Use the job to explain why buyers do or don't switch
**Use when:** interest and demos don't turn into switching, you are unsure what you really compete with, or you need the job to feed positioning, onboarding or launch messaging. For job statements, job maps and outcome statements, use `/jtbd`. This playbook only covers the GTM use of the job.  ·  **Sources:** Competing Against Luck (Christensen), Obviously Awesome (Dunford), Product-Led Growth (Bush)

## Method
1. **Get the job first.** Run `/jtbd` for the job statement and circumstance. If none exists, write a provisional one and mark it `[ASSUMPTION]` [Christensen].
2. **Interview people who switched recently and people who nearly did.** Build a timeline from their first thought that something had to change, through what they tried, to the moment they bought or gave up. Ask what they stopped using. Something always gets fired, even if it is "doing nothing" [Christensen].
3. **Map the four forces** for each story [Christensen]:
   - **Push:** the struggle in the current situation.
   - **Pull:** the appeal of the new solution.
   - **Habit:** comfort with the current way, which works against switching.
   - **Anxiety:** fear about the new choice (cost, learning, risk), which also works against it.
   Most teams work only on pull. Deals usually stall on habit and anxiety.
4. **Give each force a GTM response:**
   - **Push:** target buyers at the trigger moments that intensify it.
   - **Pull:** show the progress, not the features.
   - **Habit:** offer migration, imports, templates and side-by-side use.
   - **Anxiety:** offer a practice run, a pilot, a guarantee, references, and contracts without lock-in [Christensen] [Bush].
5. **Feed the competitive set into positioning.** Everything hired for the job counts, from any category, including nothing. That list is step 2 of `positioning.md` [Christensen] [Dunford].
6. **Track repeat use, not just the first purchase.** The purchase is the big hire. Each later use is a small hire, and only a steady run of small hires confirms you do the job. Put a repeat-use metric beside sales [Christensen].
7. **Guard the job.** A brand that does one job reliably becomes the default and earns a premium. Extensions that serve other jobs dilute it. Signal clearly who should *not* buy [Christensen].

## Rules of thumb
- If the "job" fits on a sticker ("convenience"), it is a preference, not a job. Write it with verbs and nouns, plus the circumstance [Christensen].
- Your rivals for the job should come from several categories. If they all come from one, you have described a product, not a job [Christensen].
- Where there is no real struggle, or the decision is pure arithmetic, the job lens adds little. Compete on price and convenience instead [Christensen].
- Cutting choice overload removes anxiety. Offer fewer options at the moment of decision [Christensen].

## Mistakes
- Interviewing only happy customers and never the people who nearly bought.
- Asking about features instead of the timeline.
- Adding pull (features) when anxiety is what blocks the deal.

## Validate it
- **Assumption created:** `A#: Deals in [segment] stall mainly on [anxiety/habit], and [response] removes it [ASSUMPTION, E0]`.
- **Cheapest test:** 5 switch interviews plus 3 near-switcher interviews give E1 stories. Then add the anxiety fix (a pilot, guarantee or import tool) to the next 10 opportunities and compare their conversion with the last 10. Observed movement through stages is E2, accepted pilots E3, paid conversions E4.
- **Evidence bar:** the same force shows up in at least 3 independent stories, and the response lifts paid conversion (E4). Hand the interview plan to `/customer-discovery`.
