# Pick the first segment and cross from early fans to the mainstream
**Use when:** you are choosing who to sell to first, or a few early buyers loved you and sales then stalled because new prospects want references you don't have.  ·  **Sources:** Crossing the Chasm (Moore), The Cold Start Problem (Chen), Obviously Awesome (Dunford), MOVE (Vajre)

## Method
1. **Diagnose the buyer type.** Visionaries buy a leap, tolerate gaps and decide on vision. Pragmatists buy proven productivity and want references from peers like them. If wins dry up after a few visionary deals, you have hit the chasm. A product that needs no change in behaviour may not face one [Moore].
2. **Write 8–20 one-page scenarios** from what founders and customer-facing staff already know. Each needs a header (end user, technical buyer, economic buyer); a "before" day (goal, current approach, what breaks, what it costs); and an "after" day with your product. This is a high-stakes call made on thin data, so structured judgement beats market reports [Moore].
3. **Cut with four showstoppers.** Is there an identifiable economic buyer with budget? A reason to buy strong enough that they won't wait? A whole product you can deliver within about a quarter? Is the segment free of an entrenched leader? Expect to cut about two thirds [Moore].
4. **Score the survivors** on partner availability, channel reach, price fit, positioning fit, and which neighbouring segment a win would unlock (the next bowling pin). Break ties on the strength of the reason to buy [Moore].
5. **Size it to lead it.** Your near-term target should be a big share (about half) of the segment's new purchases, and its buyers should talk to each other. If it is too big, split it along word-of-mouth lines: role, workflow, community, region [Moore] [Dunford].
6. **For networked products, choose by density.** Pick the group whose members interact most with each other, not the one with the most money at stake. For bottom-up B2B, find a dense team inside a pained segment [Chen] [Moore].
7. **Assemble the whole product.** Draw everything the buyer needs to get the promised result: integrations, data migration, training, services, compliance, support. Shade what you ship. Give each gap to a partner or to your own roadmap, and keep the set minimal. Partners join only to complete this one segment's whole product [Moore].
8. **Concentrate everything:** segment-specific references, events, vertical media, case studies and channel. Plan revenue as a staircase of segments, not a hockey stick [Moore] [Vajre].
9. **Move to the next pin** once you have several referenceable customers in the segment. Pick the adjacent segment that shares buyers, channels or whole-product pieces [Moore] [Chen].

## Rules of thumb
- One beachhead at a time. Two half-efforts are worth less than one [Moore].
- The economic cost of the problem beats the headcount of the segment [Moore].
- A segment only counts if its members reference each other when they buy. If they don't talk, word of mouth won't compound [Moore].
- Four or five customers in one segment beat one each in five [Moore].
- Winning a good-enough beachhead beats hunting for the perfect one. Decide in weeks, because time burns runway [Moore].
- Don't pick a segment from analyst forecasts [Moore].
- A sales culture that never turns down a deal cannot cross. Learn to say no [Moore] [Vajre].
- A niche doesn't have to be an industry. It can be a role, company size, workflow or geography [Moore].

## Mistakes
- Picking the largest segment, or "SMBs" in general.
- Treating visionary logos as references for pragmatists.
- Shipping the core product and leaving the buyer to assemble the rest.
- Chasing every inbound request across segments in the name of revenue.
- Expanding to a second segment before the first one refers business.

## Validate it
- **Assumption created:** `A#: [Segment] has an urgent, costly problem and an economic buyer who will pay within [N] months [ASSUMPTION, E0]`.
- **Cheapest test:** run 8–10 scenario-based interviews in the segment (`/customer-discovery`) to collect past-behaviour stories with real costs (E1). Ask to see the current workaround or spreadsheet (E2). Then ask the economic buyer for a scoped pilot with a date and named owner (E3).
- **Evidence bar:** commit when at least 3 customers in the same segment have paid (E4) and agreed to act as references (E3). Pricing claims count only at E4. If two segments tie, pick the one whose buyers talk to each other more.
