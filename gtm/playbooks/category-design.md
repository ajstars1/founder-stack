# Decide whether to create a category or fit into one
**Use when:** the product seems genuinely new, buyers keep filing you under an incumbent, or the team is excited about "creating a category".  ·  **Sources:** Play Bigger (Ramadan), Obviously Awesome (Dunford), Crossing the Chasm (Moore), 7 Powers (Helmer)

## Method
1. **Search existing frames first.** List 3–5 existing categories and subsegments you could sit in. For each, ask whether it puts your main strength at the centre and whether buyers already budget for it. Go on only if every option misrepresents you [Dunford].
2. **Test the conditions for creating one.** You need all four:
   - **Different in kind.** Comparing you with a known product produces a wrong conclusion, not just a lower score.
   - **A real shift.** A change in technology, regulation or behaviour gives a credible answer to "why now?".
   - **A felt problem with no budget line.** Buyers have the pain, but no line item for a solution exists yet. Moore warns that mainstream buyers stall without an existing budget to repurpose, so you must be able to pay for teaching them [Moore] [Ramadan].
   - **Staying power.** You can fund several concentrated pushes over one to two years and defend the lead against fast followers [Dunford] [Ramadan].
   If any condition fails, use Big Fish, Small Pond in an existing frame (`positioning.md`) and revisit in six months.
3. **Answer Peterson's three questions** in plain language: what problem, explained so a child gets it; what category you are in if you solve it perfectly; and how big that category is if you win most of it [Ramadan].
4. **Write frotos**, one from/to journey per audience (buyer, team, investor). "From" names today's world and what it costs. "To" names the new way [Ramadan].
5. **Name the category, not the product.** Use two or three words. In B2B, aim for the budget line a buyer would create, worded around the business function [Ramadan].
6. **Write the POV.** It runs problem → consequences → vision → blueprint → outcomes, with no features, in about a thousand words or fewer. Test it on outsiders: they should retell the problem, not the product. Use it as the filter for saying no to roadmap requests that drag you back toward "a better version of X" [Ramadan].
7. **Mobilize only after validation.** Set a lightning strike (one concentrated, company-wide moment) 3–6 months after the POV, then plan more within six months of each other. See `launch-strategy.md` [Ramadan] [Ries].
8. **Plan the Barrier.** A category does not defend itself. Name the Power you will build while it forms: network effects, switching costs or scale. See `moats-and-defensibility.md` [Helmer].

## Rules of thumb
- An existing frame is the default. Creating a category is the most expensive positioning style [Dunford].
- Both camps agree that creating a category means selling the problem before the product [Dunford] [Ramadan].
- **Half-step:** a new subcategory inside a known parent ("[known category] for [segment]") gets most of the clarity for a fraction of the teaching cost [Dunford].
- **Signs you need category work:** sales keeps begging for case studies, prospects send RFPs written in a competitor's terms, and every feature request gets built [Ramadan].
- Being early looks the same as being wrong. A correct problem framing still fails if buyers can't act on it yet [Ramadan].
- Play Bigger's dominance figures come from kings chosen after the fact. A failed category push can teach the market a problem that someone else then wins. Weigh the downside, not only the prize [Ramadan] [Helmer].

## Mistakes
- Inventing a category to sound large when buyers simply want a better version of what they know.
- A category name that describes your product or borrows a buzzword.
- A POV full of features, or framed as faster, cheaper or better.
- Letting customers, analysts or the press name the space for you [Ramadan].
- Going loud before the value hypothesis has evidence.

## Validate it
- **Assumption created:** `A#: Buyers in [segment] see [problem] as theirs and will adopt the [category name] framing [ASSUMPTION, E0]`.
- **Cheapest test:** read the problem statement (no product) to 8–10 target buyers. Count those who tell a specific recent story of that pain (E1) and those who show you their current workaround (E2). Then publish problem-led content or a waitlist page to targeted traffic and measure sign-ups (E2).
- **Evidence bar:** at least 3 independent design partners give scheduled time or data (E3) before any strike, and at least one pays for a pilot (E4). If buyers keep translating you back into an existing category, accept that frame. Log the result in `/facts-assumptions-unknowns`.
