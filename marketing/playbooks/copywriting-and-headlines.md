# Write headlines and copy that meet buyers where they are
**Use when:** a landing page, ad or email falls flat, a claim that used to work has died, or prospects don't believe what you say.  ·  **Sources:** Breakthrough Advertising (Schwartz), Made to Stick (Heath & Heath), Influence (Cialdini), Hacking Growth (Ellis & Brown)

## Method
1. **Choose one desire to steer** [Schwartz]: the want that scores highest on urgency, staying power and how many share it. One piece of copy, one dominant desire, one thing the product does for it. Copy can't create a want, only point an existing one at you.
2. **Diagnose awareness** [Schwartz]: how much the reader already knows. A headline for one level fails at the others.
   - *Most aware* (knows you, wants it): your name plus the offer or deal.
   - *Knows the product, not sold yet*: deepen the desire, add new proof, or update the image or mechanism.
   - *Knows the want, not your product*: name the product, prove it, and show why your way of delivering is different.
   - *Knows the problem only*: name the problem, make it vivid, and present the product as the natural way out.
   - *Unaware*: no product name and no price. Open with identification: a private wish, a quiet fear, a shared grievance, the moment of triumph, or the visible cost of the problem. A good unaware headline turns away as many readers as it pulls in.
3. **Diagnose sophistication** [Schwartz]. Read your competitors' ads to see how many claims this market has already heard.
   - *Stage 1* (you're first): a plain, direct claim.
   - *Stage 2* (the claim has been copied): a bigger, more specific version of it.
   - *Stage 3* (claims are worn out): lead with a new mechanism, meaning how it works.
   - *Stage 4* (mechanisms have been copied): an improved mechanism that is easier, faster or more certain.
   - *Stage 5* (buyers believe nothing): stop claiming and lead with who the buyer becomes.
   A mechanism has to be believable and important, not just new.
4. **Draft 10–25 headlines, varying the technique** [Ellis] [Schwartz]. Try a precise number or speed, a comparison, a metaphor, a question, a how-to, naming the problem, removing a limit, challenging a belief, newness, borrowed authority, or calling out who it isn't for. Lift customers' own words from interviews and reviews.
5. **Build belief step by step in the body** [Schwartz] [Cialdini] [Heath]:
   - Open with something the reader already accepts, and move one step at a time toward your claim.
   - Put proof where doubt shows up.
   - Explain the mechanism in plain words.
   - Offer claims the reader can check for themselves.
   - Use testimonials from people like the reader, which work best when the reader is unsure.
6. **Redefine handicaps** [Schwartz]. Rename the objection: a "repair" becomes an "adjustment", and a high price is set against the costlier standard it replaces. Sometimes the flaw itself becomes the proof.
7. **Attack an alternative only in the same breath as your fix**, and not at all if you're the market leader [Schwartz].
8. **Prove the language on traffic before scaling** [Ellis]. Language/market fit comes before spend.

## Rules of thumb
- The headline only has to earn the next line.
- Your strongest claim is often a poor headline. If readers won't believe it yet, build belief first and deliver the claim later.
- When a working claim stops working, check whether the market has moved up a stage before you reword it.
- In a sceptical market, admitting a real limitation early buys belief for everything after it.
- Use one idea per page. Three key messages land as none [Heath].
- B2B with little traffic: test headlines as cold-email subject lines or sales-call openers, and count replies.

## Mistakes
- Writing for most-aware readers when most of the traffic is cold, or the reverse.
- Copying a rival's headline. They may be at another stage or already own the claim.
- A mechanism explained in insider jargon. That's the curse of knowledge (see `sticky-messages`).
- Health, income or results claims that breach advertising or consumer law. Borrow the structure, never the claims.
- Shock headlines unrelated to the desire: clicks, not buyers.

## Template
```
Segment: ______        Dominant desire: ______
Awareness: [unaware | problem | want-not-product | product | most aware]  Evidence: ______
Sophistication stage: [1–5]  Competitor claims seen: ______
Headline mode: [plain claim | bigger claim | mechanism | better mechanism | identification]
Mechanism in one plain line: ______
Belief chain: accepted fact → ______ → ______ → claim
Proof placed at each doubt: ______
Handicap and its redefinition: ______ → ______
Headlines (10+):
1. ______   2. ______   3. ______ …
```

## Validate it
- **Assumption created:** `A#: [segment] is at awareness level X and sophistication stage Y, so headline mode Z wins [ASSUMPTION, E0]`. Read 10 competitor ads and 10 customer reviews to move it to E1.
- **Cheapest test:** run 2–4 headlines against the same traffic and the same offer, using ads, email subject lines or landing-page variants. Fix the sample size and the winning margin before you start.
- **Evidence bar:**
  - Clicks and opt-ins are E2.
  - Replies and booked calls are E3.
  - Purchases are E4.
  - A headline "people liked" in a poll is E0.
  - Write it up as an `/experiments` test card.
