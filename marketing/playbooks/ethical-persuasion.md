# Persuade without manipulating: the six levers, honestly
**Use when:** you're adding scarcity, deadlines, testimonials, notifications or habit loops, buyers show regret, or you ask "is this over the line?"  ·  **Sources:** Influence (Cialdini), Hooked (Eyal), $100M Offers (Hormozi), This Is Marketing (Godin)

## Method
1. **Name the lever at every funnel step** [Cialdini]. There are six:
   - reciprocity
   - commitment and consistency
   - social proof
   - liking
   - authority
   - scarcity
   Each one is a mental shortcut that usually serves people well, which is exactly why a fake version works and why it is a betrayal. Watch contrast effects too, since the order of options changes how each one looks.
2. **Audit every cue for truth** [Cialdini]. Use the honest form of each lever:
   - *Reciprocity*: give something useful and personal before you ask. Never use a "gift" that is really a hook with strings attached.
   - *Commitment*: ask for small, active, public and freely chosen steps, such as the customer filling in their own plan or stating a goal. Free choice matters most. Never lowball, meaning win a yes with a term you then withdraw.
   - *Social proof*: use real counts and typical testimonials from people like the buyer. It works best when buyers are unsure. Never invent reviews, and never advertise how common a bad behaviour is.
   - *Liking*: real common ground, and visible cooperation toward the buyer's goal.
   - *Authority*: relevant, checkable credentials. Admitting a small weakness raises trust in everything else you say.
   - *Scarcity*: real, explained limits, such as capacity, cohort dates or a known price rise. Newly scarce or contested items pull hardest, which is why fake versions tempt.
   - *Contrast*: show options in an honest order, for example premium first. Never show decoys you don't actually sell.
3. **Put any habit-forming product through the Manipulation Matrix before you build** [Eyal]. Answer two questions: would I use this myself, and does it materially improve users' lives?
   - Yes to both: *facilitator*. Go ahead.
   - Improves lives but you wouldn't use it: *peddler*. The odds of success are low.
   - You'd use it but it doesn't improve lives: *entertainer*. A hits business.
   - Neither: *dealer*. Don't build it.
   Even facilitators need a plan for the small share of users who overuse.
4. **Run the regret test** (a synthesis of [Cialdini] and [Eyal]). Picture a buyer who knows exactly which tactic you're using, pauses, and asks whether they would make the same choice again. If the answer is no, remove the tactic. Eyal's matrix judges the maker, and Cialdini's audit judges each cue. Use both: the matrix before building, the cue audit before shipping.
5. **Run the permission test** [Godin]. Will this message make people more or less glad to hear from you next time? Pressure tactics spend the trust you've built with your audience.
6. **Check the law.** Look up current rules where you sell: claims about health, income or results, endorsement disclosure, fake reviews, drip pricing, auto-renewal and cancellation. Cite each as `[FACT — url, date]`.

## Do-not list
- Countdown timers that reset, fake "N people viewing", and invented stock limits.
- "Valued at $X" tags nobody has ever paid.
- Fabricated testimonials, reasons or dropouts ("a spot just opened up").
- Bait-and-switch and lowballing.
- Cancellation that's deliberately harder than sign-up.
- Notifications built to exploit anxiety with no value behind them.
- Junk fees with made-up justifications.
- Targeting people in distress or with impaired judgement.

## Rules of thumb
- A real constraint is almost always available: capacity, a cohort date, a scheduled price rise. Use that instead of inventing one [Hormozi].
- Pressure lifts conversion and refunds together. Always report net kept revenue and 90-day churn next to conversion.
- In repeat relationships, trust compounds. Studies of one-off sales don't measure what a fake cue costs you later.
- People comply more when given any reason at all, so the reason you give carries a duty to be true.
- If you'd be embarrassed to explain the tactic to the customer, that's your answer.

## Mistakes
- Treating "everyone in our niche does it" as permission.
- Auditing the headline and ignoring checkout, cancellation and notifications.
- Using the matrix as self-congratulation. If you need to argue yourself into "facilitator", you aren't one.
- Optimising for conversion while ignoring refunds, chargebacks and complaints.

## Validate it
- **Assumption created:** `A#: removing [pressure tactic] does not lower net kept revenue at 90 days [ASSUMPTION, E0]`.
- **Cheapest test:** A/B test the honest version against the current one, measuring net revenue after refunds and 90-day retention, not the click.
- **Evidence bar:**
  - Net kept revenue is E4.
  - Refunds, chargebacks, complaints and unsubscribes are E2 signals of regret.
  - "Customers don't seem to mind" is E0.
  - Log the result in `/facts-assumptions-unknowns`.
