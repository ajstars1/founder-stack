# Name a company, product or feature
**Use when:** you need a new name, are checking a shortlist, or the team keeps settling on the safest option.  ·  **Sources:** Hello, My Name Is Awesome (Watkins), The 22 Immutable Laws of Branding (Ries & Ries), Zag and The Brand Gap (Neumeier), Made to Stick (Heath & Heath)

## Method
1. **Decide what you're naming, and where it sits.** A product under a strong master brand may need only a plain descriptive name. A company or standalone brand needs a proper name. Check `brand-architecture-and-extensions.md` first. [Ries & Ries]
2. **Write the creative brief, and get every decision maker to sign it first.** It covers the goal, a one-line nutshell, positioning (from `/gtm`), the audience, competitors' names, the experience you want people to have, 5–10 personality adjectives, themes to explore and avoid, five liked and five disliked names from your industry with reasons, and an acid-test sentence using the new name. [Watkins]
3. **Find the generic name for your category.** That is the one name to avoid. [Ries & Ries]
4. **Choose the name type deliberately:**
   - Descriptive: clear but forgettable, hard to protect, and soon outgrown. Use it for sub-products.
   - Suggestive or metaphor: hints at a benefit or feeling. This is the best default. [Watkins]
   - Coined or blended word: protectable, but it has to feel like a real word and needs budget to teach.
   - Founder's name: carries a story and attracts coverage, but is harder to sell or scale past the founder. [Ries & Ries]
   - Acronym: means nothing until you are huge. Avoid.
5. **Brainstorm alone, over days, not in one meeting.** Take 10–12 starter words from the brief and run each through a thesaurus, image search, industry and hobby vocabularies, idioms, titles and wordplay. Aim for at least 100 candidates. [Watkins]
6. **Screen every candidate.** Score SMILE: Suggestive, Memorable, Imagery, Legs (it can extend into a theme), Emotional. Then check SCRATCH: Spelling-challenged, Copycat, Restrictive, Annoying, Tame, Curse of knowledge, Hard to pronounce. One SCRATCH hit cuts the name. [Watkins] Cross-check against Neumeier's criteria: different, brief, apt but not generic, easy to spell and say, good for wordplay, protectable. [Neumeier]
7. **Run the sound test.** Say the name once to five people, as if over a phone, and ask them to spell it and type it. Names live as sounds before they live as logos. [Ries & Ries]
8. **Run the group review.** Circulate the list with a rationale and an example sentence for each name. Reviewers pick favourites alone, then meet to champion names rather than shoot them down. The question is whether it's right for the brand, not whether you personally like it. No focus groups. [Watkins]
9. **Clear it.** Search trademark registers in your classes and countries, check meanings and slang in each target language, and then look at domains and handles. A modifier (get-, try-, -hq, -app) is fine. A misspelling just to get the .com is not. [Watkins] Final clearance needs a trademark lawyer.
10. **Keep 3–5 finalists**, because legal screening knocks many out.

## Rules of thumb
- Memorability and easy spelling come first, and length breaks ties. Neumeier and Ries prefer short; Watkins shows a vivid long name beats a dull short one. [Watkins] [Neumeier] [Ries & Ries]
- If you would have to explain or spell the name in most conversations, keep looking.
- Name for the company you might become, not today's product or city. Still, a name so broad it evokes nothing is worse.
- Conservative categories (finance, insurance, B2B services) reward a lively name the most, because everyone else sounds alike. [Watkins]
- In multilingual markets, test pronunciation and meaning in every language and script you'll sell in. The standard checks are tuned to US English.
- Judge names spoken and in a sentence, before any logo exists.

## Mistakes
- Group consensus that drifts toward the least objectionable name. [Watkins]
- Twisting the spelling to get a .com.
- Naming the business after the category word ("Invoices Online").
- Insider jargon only your team understands. [Heath] [Watkins]
- Falling in love with a name before the trademark search.

## Validate it
- **Assumption:** `A#: target buyers can spell and recall <name> after one hearing [ASSUMPTION, E0]`.
- **Cheapest test:** mention the name once in a 30-second pitch to 10 target buyers. The next day, ask them to write it down and say what the company does. Pass if 7 of 10 spell it right and 6 of 10 recall the category: **E2**.
- **Behavioural test:** run identical small ads that differ only in the name (2–3 finalists). Click-through and signups are **E2–E3**.
- Never ask "Do you like it?" The answer is **E0**, and groups reward the safest name.
- Trademark availability counts as `[FACT]` only when it comes from the register or counsel, with URL and date.
