# Get meetings from cold outreach
**Use when:** outreach gets ignored, you need a first sequence, or you need to decide between calling, emailing and asking for referrals.  ·  **Sources:** Predictable Revenue (Ross), Gap Selling (Keenan), Founding Sales (Kazanjy), The Mom Test (Fitzpatrick), Never Split the Difference (Voss)

## Method
1. **Sell the next step, not the product.** Each touch aims at the next conversation [Kazanjy]. From a stranger that usually starts as a reply; ask for the meeting once they've engaged (`first-ask-design.md`).
2. **Pick the channel where this person actually answers.** Email only works if a personal address or a recent job-post inbox exists. Otherwise use the network where they post, or a warm intro. Shared inboxes (hello@, careers@) route to nobody [Field].
3. **Pick the entry point.**
   - **Referral-down** [Ross]: ask a senior leader who owns the problem, so the owner expects you. Use it when the problem has no obvious owner or the org is large.
   - **Direct** [Kazanjy]: write straight to the likely decision maker. Use it when the role is obvious, as in small and mid-size firms.
4. **Write the first touch** [Ross] [Keenan]. Design the ask first (`first-ask-design.md`):
   - plain text, so it reads like one person writing to another;
   - short; one specific reason now, tied to a demand signal;
   - one credibility point, such as a similar customer or a result;
   - exactly one ask.
5. **Run the value check:** what you offer minus what you ask must be positive. "30 minutes to learn about your business" is a net cost to them. A useful benchmark, teardown or answer is a gain [Keenan].
6. **Learning-stage variant** (no product to sell): ask for help, not a sale. Name the problem you're working on and your early stage, say what you're stuck on, why they in particular can help, and ask one question they can answer by reply; offer a short call only if they engage [Fitzpatrick].
7. **Choose a cadence.** Mix email, phone, voicemail and social; personalize the first touch heavily, then follow up in the same thread [Kazanjy].
   - **Burst** [Kazanjy]: about five or six touches in a week, ending with a polite sign-off. Fits a long list of mid-value accounts.
   - **Campaign** [Keenan]: 8–12 touches a few business days apart, each bringing a new impact or offer, with a more challenging note near the end. Fits a short list of valuable accounts.
8. **Follow the heat.** Call anyone who replies or clicks the same day, and answer inbound within minutes, because contact rates collapse within half an hour [Kazanjy] [Ross].
9. **On the phone:** ask permission, give your reason in one line, ask one question about their problem, and book the meeting a few days out with a same-day reminder [Kazanjy].
10. **Measure results, not activity:** replies, meetings held and qualified opportunities per 100 accounts [Ross]. Judge an angle or channel only after 5–7 touches per account over one to two weeks; most replies come after the second touch [Kazanjy] [Ross].

## Rules of thumb
- One ask per message: a referral or a time, never both [Ross].
- No fake "Re:" subjects, false familiarity or invented urgency [Ross]. They cost trust permanently.
- Don't decide for the buyer that they're too busy. Let them say no [Keenan].
- Ross's double-digit reply rates come from roughly 2003–2011 inboxes. Benchmark your own baseline.
- Deliverability, sending limits and anti-spam/privacy law (CAN-SPAM, GDPR, India's DPDP Act) change often. Check current rules by web search with `[FACT — url]`.
- [Field] A domain only weeks old: keep cold sends low (tens a day for the whole domain) and bounces under ~3%. Extra mailboxes on the same domain add no capacity, because reputation is per domain. Never guess addresses.
- [Field] Check replies twice a day, not hourly. Answer within two hours.
- Relationship-first or phone-first markets may answer a voice note or WhatsApp better than email. Treat that as an assumption and test it.
- Email copy and offer design can go to `/marketing`. This playbook owns the sequence and the ask.

## Mistakes
- Long emails that pitch features, or a demo request in touch one.
- Stopping after one or two touches.
- Sending 1,000 generic emails instead of 50 researched ones.
- Counting opens or accepted connections as interest.

## Template
```
TOUCH 1 (email). Subject: <signal> at <Company>
Hi <Name>, noticed <specific signal>. Teams like <peer> often hit <problem>
around this point, which tends to cost <impact in their terms>.
We <one-line how>; <credibility: customer/result>.
<gift: one finding about their company>. Is <problem> on your list this quarter, or not a priority?
(Once they reply: "Would it be unreasonable to spend 15 minutes on whether this applies to <Company>?")
(Referral version: "If someone else owns <problem>, who should I talk to?")

TOUCH 3 (email, same thread): <new angle: a number, a short teardown, a peer example>.
Happy to send it over. Is <problem> even on your list this quarter?

LAST TOUCH: I'll take it that <problem> isn't a priority right now and stop here.
If that changes, a one-word reply is enough and I'll send <offer>.

CALL OPENER (30s): "<me> from <co>. Did I catch you mid-something? I'm calling
because <signal>. How are you handling <problem> today?" → interested: book 2–4 days out

CADENCE
| Day | Channel | Angle / offer | Done |
```

## Validate it
- **Assumption created:** "Role R at ICP accounts responds to angle X" (`A#`, `[ASSUMPTION, E0]`).
- **Cheapest test:** two angles × 50 accounts each, same list quality, same cadence. `/experiments` can format the test card.
- **Evidence bar:** opens are not evidence. A positive reply is E2. A held meeting with an ICP contact is E3. Working-validated means E3 from ≥3 independent accounts per angle, and a clear gap in meetings per 100 between angles. Promote a reply rate to a fact (F#) only from your own sending records.
