# Pitch and demo so the buyer sees their gap closing
**Use when:** the demo is a feature tour, buyers see you as one of many, or you're building a sales deck.  ·  **Sources:** The Challenger Sale (Dixon), Gap Selling (Keenan), Founding Sales (Kazanjy), SPIN Selling (Rackham), Pitch Anything (Klaff)

## Method
**A. Choose the pitch.**
1. **Narrative pitch** (the default before ~10 customers). Problem → who carries it → cost → today's workaround and why it fails → what changed → how yours works → proof → price [Kazanjy].
2. **Teaching pitch**, once you've seen a pattern across many customers that they can't see themselves [Dixon]:
   - **Warm-up:** describe what you see at similar firms, and ask whether it rings true.
   - **Reframe:** link their known issue to a bigger, hidden problem or opportunity.
   - **The numbers:** quantify what the hidden problem costs.
   - **A story:** a similar company that lived it. This answers "we're different" better than another chart.
   - **The new way:** what they'd have to do differently, before any mention of your product.
   - **Your solution:** why you deliver that new way better than anyone.
   Build it backwards. Start from your unique strength, then ask why customers don't already value it; that answer is your reframe [Dixon].
3. **Test the reframe.** If the buyer says "exactly, that's our problem", you confirmed what they knew and taught nothing. You want visible surprise: a pause, a new question, "we've never measured it like that" [Dixon].

**B. Run the demo.**
1. **No demo without discovery.** On deals above roughly $10k, hold them on different days. On small deals, spend at least ten minutes on discovery first [Keenan] [Kazanjy].
2. **Draw a demo map.** List the problems they told you, ranked by impact. Show only the features that close those, about six at most. If it's not on the map, don't show it [Keenan].
3. **Make it theirs:** their data, their terms, their workflow, most important use case first [Kazanjy].
4. **Speak in benefits tied to needs they stated.** Feature lists early on invite price talk, and generic "this helps you…" lines invite objections. A benefit that answers a need they voiced earns agreement [Rackham].
5. **No hypotheticals.** Drop "if you…" and show what they asked for [Keenan].
6. **Check after each segment.** Ask whether it matches how they'd use it. A no means stop and dig [Keenan]. Demos suppress objections, so pause and read reactions [Dixon].
7. **Finish with proof, price, the ask.** "About us" slides go last or nowhere; lead *to* your strength, not *with* it [Dixon].

**C. Tailor by role.** For each stakeholder, write the outcome they care about (action + metric + direction), their likely objection, and your answer. Keep it on one page, so everyone can see the deal's consensus [Dixon].

## Rules of thumb
- A pitch is done when the buyer agrees an Advance, not when they applaud [Rackham].
- If they reject the problem at slide one, stop and go back to discovery. Pushing on wastes the meeting [Kazanjy].
- Teaching that doesn't lead to your unique strength is free consulting for your competitor [Dixon].
- If the decision maker interrupts, drills into minutiae or announces they have ten minutes, keep it short: top-line answers, then back to fit. See `pitching-investors.md` [Klaff].
- Keep one master deck. Use a lean version for presenting and a self-explanatory version for sending [Kazanjy].
- Positioning and category claims come from `/gtm`. Don't invent them in the deck.

## Mistakes
- Opening with company history, logos and an office map.
- Showing every feature because "they might need it".
- One demo for every buyer.
- Teaching without data you can back up. A shaky reframe costs credibility fast.
- Asking "so, what do you think?" at the end instead of proposing a next step [Kazanjy].

## Validate it
- **Assumptions created:** "The reframe is new and credible to segment S" and "features F close problems P" (`A#`, `[ASSUMPTION, E0]`).
- **Cheapest test:** run the reframe in 5 live calls and log the reaction word for word.
- **Evidence bar:** applause or "great demo" is E0. Surprise at the reframe is still words, so E1 at best. It becomes E2 when you see the buyer act on it, such as forwarding the deck internally or asking for your numbers. An agreed Advance with the decision group is E3. Working-validated means E3 from ≥3 accounts that saw the same pitch. Reframe statistics you quote need a source (`[FACT — url]`) or an internal artifact (F#). Never present an unsourced number.
