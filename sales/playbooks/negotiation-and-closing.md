# Ask for the sale, write the proposal, negotiate the terms
**Use when:** you're ready to ask, a buyer wants a proposal, procurement wants a discount, or you're haggling over price, term or a pilot.  ·  **Sources:** Never Split the Difference (Voss), Founding Sales (Kazanjy), The Challenger Sale (Dixon), Influence (Cialdini), SPIN Selling (Rackham)

## Method
1. **Ask plainly.** Recap agreed benefits, check nothing big is open, propose the biggest realistic commitment. No trick closes [Rackham] [Kazanjy]. State the price, then stop talking [Kazanjy].
2. **Propose only after they say they want to buy,** following a scoping call with the key people [Kazanjy] [Ross]. Include a short success plan: what success means, milestones, both sides' duties [Ross].
3. **Structure the offer** [Kazanjy]:
   - three options, with the middle one as your real target;
   - unit price falls only for more volume or a longer term;
   - shorter terms and instalments cost more per month;
   - your priorities, in order: cash up front, then contract length.
4. **Plan concessions.** List what you could give and want back, each scored for cost to you and value to them; know your walk-away [Dixon]. Guess their style: analyst (data, time), accommodator (relationship, hides objections) or assertive (be heard first) [Voss].
5. **When price gets attacked,** follow four steps [Dixon]:
   - Acknowledge it, and ask permission to cover other things first.
   - Put everything of value besides price on the table.
   - Ask what the discount would achieve for them.
   - Concede by plan: cheap-to-you items first, a real concession before small ones, never a small one followed by a bigger one.
6. **Use the conversational tools** [Voss]:
   - Asking how they expect you to meet a demand hands it back as their problem.
   - Labels name the tension without fighting it.
   - Usually let them name a number first.
   - When you anchor, cite a comparable deal and a range whose low end you can live with.
   - Precise numbers read as calculated.
   - Trade non-cash terms: case study, referral, logo rights, prepayment.
7. **The Ackerman model, adapted** [Voss]. As *buyer* (vendors, contractors, an acquisition): set a target, open at 65% of it, then 85%, 95%, 100%, getting a counter before each move and ending on a precise number plus a non-cash sweetener. As *seller*, mirror it: open at a credible high, concede in shrinking, traded steps [Kazanjy].
8. **Respect the anchor's limit.** Opening high then retreating invites a concession back, but a demand seen as unreasonable gets none: they stop believing you bargain in good faith [Cialdini]. Voss also warns against sharking inexperienced counterparts [Voss]. With customers you want for years, anchor with a justified range, not a shock.
9. **Make "yes" stick.** Ask how they'll know it's on track, who else signs, what happens if it slips; get the agreement three ways in one conversation [Voss].
10. **Price-match only against a verified quote, signed the same day.** Otherwise you start a reverse auction [Kazanjy].

## Rules of thumb
- Never split the difference. Trade instead [Voss].
- Every concession buys something: term, volume, prepayment, a case study, a faster signature.
- Deadlines are mostly soft, theirs included [Voss].
- Get ahead of "fair" by inviting them to tell you if anything feels unfair [Voss].
- If a pilot is the close, it needs a price on success and a dated decision meeting.

## Mistakes
- Discounting before the buyer asks.
- Sending pricing to someone who hasn't done discovery.
- Conceding in growing steps, which teaches them to keep pushing.
- Treating procurement's first number as final.
- Ending on "so, what do you think?" instead of a proposal [Klaff] [Kazanjy].
